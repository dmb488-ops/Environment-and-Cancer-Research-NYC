    Result: which factors do the methods agree on?

    How the verdict is decided

    Each method is a different way of asking "is this factor associated with incidence?". A method supports a factor when:

    • Spearman screen: rank correlation with the outcome has p < 0.05 (55 neighborhoods).
    • OLS + smoking: the factor still has p < 0.05 after adjusting for smoking alone, so it must add something beyond smoking.
    • OLS, Poisson (age strata), panel two-way fixed effects: p < 0.05 with robust standard errors, in a model that holds only the low-collinearity core set of factors.
    • Elastic Net: the factor keeps a non-zero coefficient in at least 60% of 400 random half-samples of the neighborhoods (stability selection).
    • Random Forest, Gradient Boosting: shuffling the factor makes predictions for held-out neighborhoods at least 1 R² point worse, in at least 80% of the cross-validation folds.

    A factor is Selected only when (a) at least 60% of the methods that could test it support it, with a consistent direction; (b) its scientific case is strong or moderate; (c) the direction agrees with theory; and (d) the machine-learning support is not a location fingerprint: for a factor that mostly separates the boroughs, its importance must survive holding a whole borough out. Everything else is a Flag (a statistical signal that fails a scientific or robustness check), Hypothesis only (plausible mechanism, no independent support in these data) or Not supported.

    Explore first: what does each factor look like?

    Before any model, look at every factor on its own. Spatial share is how much of a factor's variation is real difference between neighborhoods (the rest is change over the years). Borough share is how much a neighborhood's borough alone explains: a high value means the factor mostly says where a neighborhood is, not what it is like. ρ is a Spearman rank correlation with incidence: +1 means neighborhoods that rank high on the factor also rank high on incidence, 0 means no relation. The bar shows a 95% bootstrap interval; click a row to see its scatter plot.

    2

    Multicollinearity: do the factors overlap?

    What it means. Multicollinearity happens when two or more factors move together so closely that a regression cannot tell them apart. NO₂, NO, black carbon and PM₂.₅ all come from the same traffic and heating sources, so wherever one is high the others are too. Put them all in one regression and the model cannot decide which one deserves the credit: the estimates become unstable, the confidence intervals balloon, and a factor can even change sign depending on which others are included. That makes the results impossible to interpret, even though the model still predicts well.

    How we measure it. The correlation matrix shows which pairs overlap. The variance inflation factor (VIF) asks, for each factor, how well the other factors can predict it: VIF = 1 / (1 − R²). A VIF of 5 means the uncertainty of its coefficient is about √5 ≈ 2.2 times larger than it would be if the factor stood alone; 5–10 is the usual warning level. The condition number summarises the whole set (above about 30 is a warning).

    What we do about it. Keep one well-justified representative from each group of near-duplicates, drop factors with no scientific case, and run the regressions on the resulting core set. Tree-based machine learning tolerates correlated inputs for prediction, but its importance scores are shared out arbitrarily between look-alikes, so the page also reports grouped importance (whole themes shuffled together).

    3

    Theory: is there a plausible reason?

    Statistical significance or importance alone is not enough. Each factor is rated by how strong the scientific case is that it affects lung-cancer incidence, with the direction theory expects. A factor is only selected when the data agree. This is an initial draft to be checked together; sources are listed in the methods document.

    4

    Models: how well do they predict, and which factors drive them?

    Regression models (OLS, Poisson, panel fixed effects) give an effect size with a confidence interval but need the low-collinearity core set. Machine-learning models (Elastic Net, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) can use all 16 factors and capture curves and interactions; they answer a prediction question — how much worse are predictions for neighborhoods the model has not seen if this factor is shuffled? Two tests of "unseen": random 5-fold (whole neighborhoods held out at random) and leave-one-borough-out (predict a whole borough from the other four), a harder test that exposes variables that only act as a location fingerprint.

    Predictive performance (cross-validated R²)

    R² = share of the variation between unseen neighborhoods that the model predicts. 0 = no better than guessing the city average; negative = worse. Comparing feature sets shows what each group of factors adds on top of the others.

    !

    Read these before quoting a result

    • Neighborhoods, not people. Every number describes areas (the ecological design). A neighborhood-level association does not say what happens to an individual.
    • Only 55 observations. With so few neighborhoods, models with many variables overfit; the regressions use at most seven factors and the machine-learning scores are cross-validated.
    • Smoking is a proxy. The survey measures current adult smoking; lung cancer today reflects smoking decades ago. Smoking is also the strongest confounder, so the real question is what environmental factors add beyond smoking.
    • Mismatched geographies. Smoking, insurance and greenness come from 34 UHF areas and air pollution from 59 community districts; both are blended onto the 55 neighborhoods, which hides local variation and can weaken real associations.
    • Neighbors resemble each other. Nearby neighborhoods tend to have similar rates, which can make p-values too optimistic. Moran's I of the model residuals is shown in step 4.
    • Not measured: residential history, immigration and occupation, radon, second-hand smoke, screening intensity. Differences in detection (insurance, screening) can mimic risk factors.
    • Association is not causation. "Selected" means consistently associated and scientifically plausible, which is a reason to study a factor further, not proof that it causes cancer.
